Types of Sensory Receptor & Transduction

Biological Transducer: Every sensory receptor is specific to one type of stimulus and converts its energy (mechanical, chemical, light, thermal) into electrical energy (a generator potential).

  • Mechanoreceptors (e.g., Pacinian corpuscle in skin / baroreceptors in arteries): Detect mechanical pressure or stretch. Deformation opens stretch-mediated Na⁺ channels.
  • Chemoreceptors (e.g., olfactory cells in nose, taste buds, carotid/aortic bodies): Detect specific chemical molecules or blood pH/CO₂ levels.
  • Thermoreceptors (e.g., hypothalamus & skin endings): Detect changes in internal blood temperature or external ambient temperature.
  • Photoreceptors (e.g., rods and cones in the retina): Detect light intensity and wavelength (rods: low light, rhodopsin, monochrome; cones: bright light, iodopsin, colour, high acuity).

Neurone Types & Reflex Arcs

Sensory Neurone: Carries impulses from receptor → CNS. Has a long dendron, cell body positioned off to the side in the dorsal root ganglion, and a shorter axon; usually myelinated.

Relay (Intermediate) Neurone: Lies entirely within the CNS (brain/spinal cord) linking sensory to motor neurones. Has many short dendrites and a short axon; usually unmyelinated.

Motor Neurone: Carries impulses from CNS → effector (muscle/gland). Has a large cell body at one end inside the CNS with short dendrites and a long myelinated axon ending in motor end plates.

Reflex Arc Pathway: Stimulus → Receptor → Sensory neurone → (Relay neurone in CNS) → Motor neurone → Effector → Rapid involuntary response. Note: the knee-jerk reflex is monosynaptic (no relay neurone, only 1 synapse) for ultra-fast posture adjustment, whereas the corneal/blinking reflex uses a relay neurone in the pons.

Conduction Speed & Spike Frequency Maths

Conduction Speed (m s⁻¹) = Distance (m) ÷ Time (s)  |  Frequency (Hz) = 1 ÷ Period (s)

Unit Trap: Oscilloscope traces almost always show time in milliseconds (ms)! Divide ms by 1,000 to convert to seconds (e.g., 4 ms = 0.004 s). If an impulse travels 0.36 m in 4 ms, speed = 0.36 ÷ 0.004 = 90 m s⁻¹.

Three Factors Increasing Conduction Speed: (1) Myelination (saltatory conduction jumps node to node, up to 120 m s⁻¹ vs ~2 m s⁻¹ unmyelinated), (2) Wider axon diameter (less cytoplasmic resistance to ion flow/local currents), (3) Higher temperature (faster ion diffusion and ATP production for Na⁺/K⁺ pumps, up to enzyme denaturation point).
